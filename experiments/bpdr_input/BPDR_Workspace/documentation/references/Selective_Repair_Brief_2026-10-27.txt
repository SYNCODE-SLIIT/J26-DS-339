# Research-backed plan and implementation prompt for BPDR selective vocal repair

## Executive recommendation

Based on the state of your project and recent work on automatic singing pitch correction, I would **keep the proposed direction but tighten it considerably**:

> **First make the existing Transformer produce verified, audible local repairs. Then add “Selective Vocal Repair with Protected Regions” as the visible new feature for the presentation.**

This is a better direction than adding another music-analysis capability. Recent automatic pitch-correction research consistently treats preservation of expressive pitch variation—vibrato, bends, transitions, glides—as a central problem rather than simply minimizing pitch error. Continuous corrections have specifically been investigated to avoid the unnatural behavior produced by snapping a voice to discrete note centers. citeturn3view1turn4view4

A particularly relevant 2026 system, BERT-APC, explicitly separates the idea of the perceived/stationary pitch from expressive fluctuations and corrects pitch while attempting to retain intentional deviations. Its authors also evaluate the **pitch of the actually corrected audio**, rather than stopping at target-pitch prediction accuracy. citeturn4view0turn4view2 That distinction is directly relevant to your current situation: according to your project results, **5.84 cents prediction error is encouraging, but it does not prove that the delivered WAV is improved**.

I would **not**, however, copy BERT-APC's music-language-model or musical-context inference into BPDR. BERT-APC reasons about note sequences and musical context; in your team architecture that starts moving toward the melody-understanding and harmonic components. citeturn3view0 Your component can take the safer boundary:

**BPDR predicts and executes local waveform repair. It does not decide notes, beats, keys, chords, or phrases.**

The proposed presentation feature therefore becomes:

> **Selective Vocal Repair with Protected Regions**  
> BPDR proposes verified local audio corrections. The user can protect intentional expressive regions, preview individual corrections, accept/reject them, and export only a final combination that passes the same technical verification.

That gives you something visibly new without inventing a second unrelated main feature.

## What the research suggests changing

### Prediction quality should no longer be the main success metric

Your present result of **5.84 cents** indicates that the Transformer has learned a useful correction signal, assuming that number was obtained on the evaluation setup you described. The next bottleneck is therefore likely in the path:

**Transformer → region decision → permission policy → renderer → output WAV → output verification**

Recent pitch-correction research supports evaluating this full path. For example, BERT-APC separately extracted pitch from its corrected singing audio to determine whether the synthesized result actually followed the intended pitch; the reported corrected-waveform result was lower than the upstream target-pitch prediction result because expressive variations and rendering affect the final measurement. citeturn4view2

Therefore, do **not** optimize only:

```text
predicted correction vs. target correction
```

The core metric should become:

```text
pitch error in input WAV
        ↓
Transformer proposal
        ↓
rendered output WAV
        ↓
pitch error measured again
```

A candidate is successful only when the **rendered output** improves.

This is also why your current observation—good prediction error but no improvement in scored shifted regions—is important rather than contradictory. It suggests that prediction, eligibility policy, or audio realization is blocking the improvement.

### Preserve expression instead of forcing a perfectly flat pitch contour

Smooth, context-aware pitch correction is not a new concern. Earlier work explicitly modeled correction as a continuous quantity in frequency and time so that pitch bending and vibrato could survive correction. citeturn3view1 Deep AutoTuner similarly trained using both erroneous intonation and intentional pitch variation, with the latter intended to be preserved. citeturn4view4

More recent singing research gives an even stronger technical clue. VibE-SVC and VibE-SVC2 explicitly decompose F0 information into lower- and higher-frequency components, using the high-frequency contour to represent aspects of vibrato style. citeturn3view5

That means I would avoid a training loss such as:

```text
make correction completely constant inside every repair interval
```

because it can produce the wrong inductive bias.

Instead, train the model to predict the **detuning correction**, while preserving fine expressive variation already present in the voice.

A reasonable loss design is:

\[
L =
\lambda_{repair} L_{repair}
+
\lambda_{identity} L_{identity}
+
\lambda_{gradient} L_{gradient}
+
\lambda_{boundary} L_{boundary}
\]

where:

**Repair loss**

\[
L_{repair} =
Huber(\hat c_t,c_t)
\]

on frames that genuinely contain introduced pitch errors.

**Identity loss**

\[
L_{identity} =
Huber(\hat c_t,0)
\]

on clean/original frames.

This directly teaches:

> “When no repair is required, predict no correction.”

That is much better than hoping preservation emerges indirectly.

**Gradient/continuity loss**

\[
L_{gradient} =
Huber(
(\hat c_t-\hat c_{t-1}),
(c_t-c_{t-1})
)
\]

This encourages the *shape* of the correction to behave consistently with the target instead of simply minimizing framewise error.

That distinction matters. A plain total-variation loss on the prediction could accidentally reward overly flat correction curves. Matching the target derivative provides continuity without automatically suppressing legitimate temporal structure.

**Boundary loss**

Penalize non-zero correction immediately outside a repair interval and/or train with tapered correction targets around edit boundaries.

This should reduce the sudden discontinuities that can otherwise become audible when edited audio is inserted back into the original waveform.

This exact combined objective is my design recommendation rather than something copied from one paper, but its components follow the consistent research direction of continuous correction, identity preservation, and explicit handling of expressive pitch structure. citeturn3view1turn4view4turn3view5

### Improve the renderer before increasing Transformer size

I would **not increase the Transformer size yet**.

The evidence you supplied says the model is already predicting corrections with relatively small error, while the allowed/editable path fails to produce corresponding delivered improvements. Adding parameters does not directly solve policy fragmentation, edit boundaries, formant distortion, or verification failures.

The renderer should therefore become a first-class component.

If the project currently uses `librosa.phase_vocoder` directly, that is especially worth revisiting. Librosa's own documentation describes its phase vocoder as a simplified implementation, notes that it does not specially handle transients, warns that audible artifacts are likely, and points users toward Rubber Band for higher-quality processing. citeturn3view4

Rubber Band is particularly relevant for vocal repair because its API supports **formant-preserved pitch shifting**, and its high-consistency mode is specifically intended for dynamically changing pitch shifts without discontinuities. citeturn3view3

I would therefore benchmark:

```text
existing renderer
vs.
Rubber Band / pyrubberband with formant preservation
```

on exactly the same repaired regions.

Do not automatically replace the existing implementation just because another library exists. If the current renderer performs better in your tests, retain it. But for a singing-voice system, formant-preserving rendering is valuable enough to test before spending time retraining a larger model. citeturn3view3

### Do not make the permission system frame-by-frame

Your description that useful predictions are being rejected suggests that the current policy may be too fragmented.

A vocal repair is naturally a **time span**, even if the Transformer predicts framewise values.

Turn the predictions into objects such as:

```json
{
  "repair_id": "repair_003",
  "start_sec": 4.82,
  "end_sec": 5.41,
  "mean_correction_cents": -27.6,
  "max_abs_correction_cents": 34.2,
  "confidence": 0.91,
  "status": "verified",
  "protected": false,
  "accepted": true
}
```

The regionizer should:

```text
frame predictions
      ↓
confidence/voicing filtering
      ↓
hysteresis
      ↓
merge short gaps
      ↓
reject technical outliers
      ↓
produce contiguous repair candidates
```

Do not independently switch correction on and off every few frames.

This idea is also consistent with recent APC research emphasizing meaningful stable regions rather than treating every fluctuating F0 frame equally. BERT-APC, for example, explicitly gives lower importance to transitional or highly fluctuating areas when estimating a representative pitch. citeturn4view2

Your implementation does **not** need to create musical notes to get the same engineering benefit. It can simply create **audio repair intervals**.

## Proposed BPDR architecture and feature boundary

The cleanest system would look like this:

```text
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
Original vocal WAV ─────►│ Existing preprocessing  │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
                         │ Transformer             │
                         │ Δ cents + confidence    │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
                         │ Repair Regionizer       │
                         │ contiguous candidates   │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                              subtract protected
                                  user regions
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
                         │ Technical Policy Gate   │
                         │ limits / support / etc. │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
                         │ Local Pitch Renderer    │
                         │ formants + boundaries   │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                                      ▼
                         ┌─────────────────────────┐
                         │ Delivered-WAV Verifier  │
                         └────────────┬────────────┘
                                      │
                           pass       │       fail
                           ┌──────────┴──────────┐
                           ▼                     ▼
                     candidate shown         rollback /
                     to user                 unchanged
                           │
                     accept/reject
                           │
                           ▼
                    FINAL RENDER FROM
                    ORIGINAL WAV
                           │
                           ▼
                    FINAL VERIFICATION
                           │
                           ▼
                       Export WAV
```

The important architectural rule is:

> **Every final export should be reconstructed from the original WAV plus the accepted repair specification—not by repeatedly modifying an already modified WAV.**

That prevents errors and artifacts from accumulating when the user previews, rejects, re-enables, or rearranges edits.

### Protected regions

Protection should be represented simply as intervals:

```json
{
  "protected_regions": [
    {
      "id": "protect_001",
      "start_sec": 7.16,
      "end_sec": 8.02,
      "label": "Keep original"
    }
  ]
}
```

Protected regions should participate in policy as:

```text
effective_edit_mask =
technical_allowed_mask
AND model_supported_mask
AND NOT protected_mask
```

The crucial rule is:

> **User permission can only remove edits. It can never force an unsafe edit through the technical gate.**

So:

```text
User says KEEP ORIGINAL
→ guaranteed no correction.

User says ACCEPT REPAIR
→ repair is still allowed only if verification passes.
```

That gives the feature a defensible safety model.

### Individual repair review

Each verified candidate should display only information BPDR owns:

```text
Repair 2
4.82 s – 5.41 s

Suggested adjustment:
−28 cents

Status:
Verified

[Original] [Repaired]

[Accept repair]
[Keep original]
```

Avoid displaying:

```text
Detected note: C#4
Correct note: D4
Key: A major
Beat: 3
Chord: B minor
Phrase: ...
```

because those values invade your teammates' responsibilities.

The timeline can still display a pitch curve if you already have one internally, but label the objects:

**repair regions**

rather than:

**incorrect notes**.

### Repair audit information

One small companion feature would make the presentation much stronger without expanding scope: expose **why BPDR did or did not repair something**.

For example:

```text
Repair proposed
Verified
User accepted
```

or:

```text
No repair applied
Reason: insufficient model support
```

or:

```text
No repair applied
Reason: protected by user
```

or:

```text
Repair rolled back
Reason: delivered pitch did not improve
```

This is particularly useful because your system is intentionally conservative. Instead of an unchanged region appearing to be a failure, the demo can visibly show **why it remained unchanged**.

I would include this if it is inexpensive, but it should remain secondary to getting the delivered repairs working.

## Development and evaluation plan

I would restructure your original five phases slightly.

### Repair-path diagnosis

Before retraining anything, create a trace for every candidate.

For each shifted test interval record:

```text
ground-truth shift
Transformer predicted correction
prediction MAE
prediction confidence
candidate start/end
candidate permitted?
if rejected → exact reason
render attempted?
output path
output pitch error before
output pitch error after
outside-region preservation
final disposition
```

One machine-readable artifact could be:

```json
{
  "sample": "singer_07_clip_12",
  "region": [2.41, 3.08],
  "true_shift_cents": 40.0,
  "predicted_median_cents": -36.7,
  "prediction_mae_cents": 4.8,
  "policy": {
    "permitted": false,
    "reason": "insufficient_support"
  },
  "rendered": false,
  "verified": false
}
```

Then classify every failure into:

```text
prediction failure
regionization failure
permission failure
renderer failure
verification failure
```

Do not start Phase Two until the dominant category is known.

This could reveal, for example, that a Transformer whose correction is fundamentally correct is being broken into many short spans and rejected for insufficient support. In that situation, retraining would attack the wrong layer.

### Prediction and region quality

Only after diagnosis should you modify training.

My preferred experiment order is:

```text
Baseline current Transformer

A. + identity / zero-correction loss

B. + correction-gradient continuity loss

C. + boundary-aware training

D. + A+B+C

Optional:
E. low/high-frequency F0 treatment if vibrato preservation remains poor
```

Do not change several other variables simultaneously.

Use singer-held-out or source-held-out development folds where possible. In particular, do not tune the model, renderer thresholds, region merging, and permission thresholds repeatedly against the evaluation set you intend to cite as final evidence.

Keep the already-inspected calibration examples as **development evidence**, as you suggested.

Once a configuration is frozen, run it on a fresh holdout.

### Rendering and verification

Every permitted region should go through:

```text
original region
    ↓
pitch shift
    ↓
boundary transition / crossfade
    ↓
insert into original timeline
    ↓
remeasure delivered waveform
```

For a local repair, verification should answer at least:

**Did the targeted region improve?**

```text
error_after < error_before
```

preferably by a meaningful margin determined using development data.

**Was untouched material preserved?**

Where samples are intentionally untouched, waveform difference should ideally be zero outside the edit plus crossfade margin if your architecture supports literal copying.

**Did timing remain unchanged?**

```text
len(output_samples) == len(input_samples)
```

unless your BPDR specification explicitly allows time modification.

**Were pauses preserved?**

No voiced material should unexpectedly appear in unvoiced sections.

**Was the output damaged?**

Check for NaN/Inf, clipping introduced by processing, empty channels, failed render, channel-count changes, and sample-rate changes.

On any hard failure:

```text
rollback to original
```

rather than shipping the questionable repair.

For pitch tracking, CREPE remains a well-established neural monophonic pitch estimator and was specifically evaluated for noise robustness. citeturn1search0 More recent estimators such as SwiftF0 report substantial efficiency and robustness improvements, including evaluation on held-out audio domains. citeturn1search23 I would not replace your existing pitch estimator immediately; a useful research check is to compare a subset with a second estimator so the entire pipeline is not judged only by exactly the same F0 algorithm used upstream.

### Selective review implementation

Only after one or more repairs can reliably survive the delivered-WAV verifier should you add the UI.

The minimum usable feature is:

```text
timeline
+
proposed repair regions
+
drag-select "Keep original"
+
A/B preview for candidate
+
accept
+
reject
+
final export
```

The UI does **not** need to become a Melodyne-style note editor.

That would substantially increase implementation effort and blur your team boundary.

A presentation-quality timeline can be much simpler:

```text
0s                                                     12s
│────────────────────────────────────────────────────────│
        █████                  ▒▒▒▒▒          ███
        Repair                 Protected       Repair
         ✓                      Keep             ×
```

Where:

```text
Repair ✓ = accepted verified repair
Protected = user-protected original
Repair × = rejected repair
```

### Evidence and success gates

Do not report only one aggregate error.

I would report at least these quantities separately.

| Measurement | What it answers |
|---|---|
| Prediction MAE in cents | Does the Transformer know the correction? |
| Delivered region error before/after | Did the resulting WAV actually improve? |
| Verified repair success rate | How often do attempted repairs succeed? |
| Useful coverage | How much eligible erroneous material gets successfully repaired? |
| Rejection rate by reason | Why are corrections being withheld? |
| Outside-region preservation | Did local repair damage untouched material? |
| Unchanged-original control | Does a good input remain good? |
| Duration/sample-count integrity | Did rendering alter timing? |
| Protected-region preservation | Does the new feature actually prevent editing? |

I strongly recommend **useful coverage** because otherwise a conservative system can obtain excellent preservation scores simply by editing nothing.

For example:

\[
UsefulCoverage =
\frac{\text{duration of successfully delivered repair}}
{\text{duration of eligible shifted material}}
\]

Then pair it with:

\[
RepairSuccessRate =
\frac{\text{verified improved repairs}}
{\text{attempted repairs}}
\]

The presentation target is not necessarily 100% coverage.

A defensible BPDR story is:

> “BPDR corrects regions when there is sufficient evidence and leaves uncertain or intentionally protected material unchanged.”

For final perceptual testing, standardized audio-quality methodology exists: ITU-R BS.1534 defines MUSHRA for subjective assessment of intermediate audio quality. citeturn3view6 A full MUSHRA experiment is probably unnecessary for your immediate progress presentation, but the underlying lesson is useful: **objective pitch accuracy and perceived audio quality should not be treated as the same measurement**. Recent APC research likewise reports subjective expression-preservation alongside pitch-correction accuracy. citeturn4view2

7. Silence/pause preservation where applicable.
8. sample rate/channel integrity.
9. NaN/Inf/empty audio checks.
10. clipping or other obvious rendering failure checks.

A repair that fails a hard verification condition must be rolled back to the
original audio.

Keep verification thresholds in configuration.
Do not invent evaluation thresholds only to make the current model pass.

Store results such as:

If practical, compare some verification examples using a second pitch estimator

==================================================

Implement protected regions as audio time intervals.

Example:

    id
    start_time
    end_time
    label: "Keep original"
}

Effective editing permission should conceptually be:

effective_allowed =
    model_supported
    AND technical_policy_allowed
    AND NOT protected_by_user

Protected regions must NOT:
- alter the Transformer's musical prediction,
- become melody notes,
- change another team's analysis,
- bypass technical policy.

They simply prevent BPDR from changing specified samples/time intervals.

Persist protected intervals in the existing project/session format in the least
invasive way possible.
