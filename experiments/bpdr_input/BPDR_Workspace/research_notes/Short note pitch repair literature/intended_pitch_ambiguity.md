# Intended-pitch ambiguity in sung/hummed notes (10-30 cent deviations)

Verification key: [P] = I read the primary text (PDF) myself. [S] = secondary/snippet only, not verified against the primary. [U] = could not verify.

## 1. Perceptual thresholds: when is a sung or short note "out of tune"?

### Takeaway
Listener tolerance for sung pitch ranges from about 10 cents (expert musicians, careful method-of-limits tests) to about 50 cents (typical "out of tune" criterion in singing research). Even 40 cent errors are not reliably judged out of tune by most listeners. Natural singer error is about 19-25 cents mean absolute, so a 10-30 cent deviation lies inside both the perceptual tolerance band and the singer's own production noise. A deviation of that size cannot be called an error without knowing the intended target, the musical context and the listener.

### Cited Findings
- Mauch, Frieler & Dixon (2014, J. Acoust. Soc. Am. 136(1):401-411) [P]: the median singer error is 19 cents. The mean absolute pitch error (MAPE) per recording is about 0.19 semitones (mean 0.189, median 0.187, SD 0.051). They note that 20 cents is "slightly larger than the just noticeable difference", which for typical singing frequencies up to 800 Hz is usually <1%, i.e. <17 cents (citing Henning 1966). — [Mauch et al. 2014 PDF](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2014/JASA_136_1_401_1.pdf)
- Same paper [P], citing Vurma & Ross (2006): professional singers had SD 22 cents in interval size and 34 cents in absolute pitch against a tuning-fork reference. Judgments of out-of-tune singing correlated with pitch error, but "errors of even 40 cents were not reliably judged out of tune by the majority of listeners". Immediately after singing, the singers themselves could not judge whether their intervals were out of tune. — [Mauch et al. 2014 PDF](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2014/JASA_136_1_401_1.pdf)
- Larrouy-Maestri, Gosselin, Blanckaert & Morsomme, "Listeners' tolerance when listening to melodic performances" (ESCOM 2015 slides, Univ. of Liege) [P, slides only]:
  - 30 musicians and 30 non-musicians used a method-of-limits "in tune / out of tune" judgement on familiar and unfamiliar melodies.
  - Test-retest correlation was r = .91.
  - There was a large expertise effect (F(1,116) = 139.11, p < .001, eta-squared .54), no familiarity effect and no effect of deviation direction in the main group.
  - Their conclusion is "Low tolerance, particularly for music experts" and a "Limit around 10 cents, even with very precise material". Deviations tested ran from 0 to 60 cents in 10 cent steps, then 0 to 30 cents in 5 cent steps for musicians.
  - Slide text summarizes earlier criteria as semitone (100 cents) in the Dalla Bella and Pfordresher line of work, and quartertone (50 cents) in Hutchins & Peretz 2012, Hutchins, Roquet & Peretz 2012, and Pfordresher & Mantell 2014. It also states that lay tolerance for unfamiliar melodies is "much less than a quartertone".
  - I did not find the exact per-group mean cent values in the text. A metadata abstract mentioned about 20 cents as a deviation still rated "in tune" [S/U]. — [slides PDF](https://orbi.uliege.be/bitstream/2268/186233/2/2015_ESCOM_tolerance.pdf)
- Hutchins, Roquet & Peretz (2012, "The Vocal Generosity Effect: How Bad Can Your Singing Be?", Music Perception) [S]: secondary sources say listeners judge voice more leniently than instruments, and that most listeners do not notice tuning errors in the voice until about 50 cents. I did not read the paper itself. — [Frontiers 2015 review, PMC4432667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4432667) and [Springer 2014 (s13414-014-0732-1)](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.3758/s13414-014-0732-1.pdf) (snippets only)
- The "poor-pitch singer" cutoff varies by study [S]: 100 cents in Pfordresher & Brown (2007), 50 cents in Demorest & Clements (2007) and Hutchins & Peretz (2012). Dalla Bella (2015) proposes a relative cutoff of 2 SD from a normative group. Poor-pitch singers did not differ from good singers in pitch discrimination, which suggests a production (sensorimotor mapping) deficit rather than a perception deficit. Prevalence is about 10-15% (contested). — [Frontiers Psychol. 2013 article](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2013.00714/full), [PMC4432667](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4432667) (snippet-level)
- Duration dependence: Moore (1973, JASA 54:610-619, "Frequency difference limens for short-duration tones") argues the frequency difference limen scales roughly as Δf·d ≥ constant for tones below about 4 kHz, so discrimination gets worse as the tone gets shorter [S, abstract level]. A separate study of 1-3 cycle tones at 256-2048 Hz reported limens on the order of 1-2 semitones [S]. I could not obtain Moore's per-duration numbers in cents. — [Scilit record](https://scilit.com/publications/6f6db57229ad1e3aa2cd59f25bdfb96f), [IOA paper on audibility of transient pitch shifts](https://www.ioa.org.uk/system/files/publications/JAS%20ANGUS%20CM%20BARNES%20ON%20THE%20AUDIBILITY%20OF%20TRANSIENT%20PITCH%20SHIFTS.pdf)
- Vibrato: for long vibrato tones, the perceived pitch tracks the mean (arithmetic or geometric) of the F0 over a vibrato cycle (Sundberg; Shonle & Horan, modulations up to 200 cents). Castellengo and colleagues report that for short vibrato tones the perceived pitch can be far from the mean of the extremes. In Sundberg's operatic-intonation work, a tolerance zone of about ±10 cents around the target was reported for the mean frequency. These are all secondary readings of snippets [S]. — [Castellengo 1994, short-duration vibrato tones](https://www.lam.jussieu.fr/Membres/Castellengo/publications/1994d-Short-duration%20Vibrato%20tone.pdf), [Sundberg-related page](https://pressto.amu.edu.pl/index.php/ism/article/download/15017/14759/30310)

### Inferences
- A 10-30 cent offset sits at or below the 50 cent "out of tune" criterion. It is at or above the roughly 10-17 cent JND and the roughly 10 cent expert limit. Whether it counts as an error therefore depends on the listener and criterion, not on the signal. The audio evidence alone only yields a deviation relative to some assumed grid.
- Because discrimination degrades for very short tones (Moore) and perceived pitch of short vibrato tones is not simply the mean, short-note pitch is perceptually less precise. This supports being conservative about "repairing" short notes. The inference is mine, from abstract-level sources.

### Gaps
- Moore 1973 limens in cents at 10, 20 and 50 ms were not found. No study directly measuring "in tune" judgements as a function of sung-note duration was found.
- I did not read Hutchins et al. 2012 or Pfordresher & Brown 2007 primary texts.
- Context effects (key and tonal context shifting the tolerated deviation) were not retrieved with numbers.

## 2. How large is natural expressive deviation in singing and humming?

### Takeaway
Untrained to semi-trained singers deviate from an equal-tempered grid by about 0.19-0.25 semitones (19-25 cents) mean absolute per note, with systematic, context-predictable biases of 15-30 cents (phrase onsets, repeated notes, large intervals) and slow whole-song drift of about 11 cents (median) over about 50 s. Datasets that annotate notes (Vocadito, HumTrans, MIR-ST500) do so by human judgement or reference scores, not by measured pitch alone.

### Cited Findings
- Mauch et al. 2014 [P] (24 singers selected as non-"poor", 72 recordings, 4789 notes of "Happy Birthday"; the 24 are the remainder after excluding 7 of 31 recruits, 4 of them as outlier "poor" singers):
  - Median absolute intonation drift was 11 cents over about 50 s. Drift was significant in 22% of recordings (p < 0.01), or 38% at p < 0.05.
  - Drift did not correlate with other accuracy measures or singing experience.
  - Repeated same-pitch notes (the prime interval) were sung sharp by about 0.29 semitones on average.
  - Large upward intervals (8 and 12 semitones) were compressed (flat).
  - Phrase-initial notes were about 21 cents flat. Each signed semitone of following interval added about 1.3 cents sharpening.
  - Together these two effects explain only 9.8% of the variance in interval error.
  - Equal temperament fit better than just intonation, but by only 1.3 cents mean absolute pitch error.
  - Notes were scored using the median F0 over the annotated stable part. Annotators who included transitions versus those who didn't produced no significant difference in the median.
  - — [Mauch et al. 2014 PDF](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2014/JASA_136_1_401_1.pdf)
- Dai & Dixon (2017, ISMIR, "Analysis of Interactive Intonation in Unaccompanied SATB Ensembles") [P, partial read]: MAPE tables for isolated singers in a quartet are 0.2484 (soprano), 0.2483 (alto), 0.2328 (tenor) and 0.2082 (bass). The table does not state units on the line I read. By analogy with Mauch et al. these are semitones, so about 21-25 cents, but this is my inference. Ensemble interaction changed MAPE by only a few cents (for example 2-4 cents differences between conditions). The setup was 20 participants in 5 groups of 4. — [Dai & Dixon ISMIR 2017](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2017/paper/000024.pdf)
- Dai & Dixon (2019, JASA quartet study) [S]: harmonic intervals tend to be expanded by about 8 cents between adjacent voices. — [DaiDixon JASA Quartet](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2019/DaiDixon-JASA-Quartet.pdf) (snippet only)
- Vocadito (Bittner et al., 2021, arXiv 2110.05580; dataset report, not a conference paper per what I could confirm) [P]:
  - 40 monophonic singing excerpts, f0 plus two independent note annotations, with lyrics.
  - The annotators were told to label "the notes they would play if they had to reproduce it on the piano". Tony allows continuous pitch because singers "did not necessarily sing in standard tuning (440 Hz)".
  - Annotator agreement on notes was low: 0.64 F-measure on average, and 0.34 for the worst track.
  - Short notes and ornaments were the main discrepancy: Annotator 1 labeled many grace notes and ornaments as separate notes, Annotator 2 grouped them.
  - Quantizing the ground-truth f0 to the nearest semitone and merging runs gives Acc 0.55, F 0.20 and Fno 0.38, so it does not give a reasonable note estimate.
  - — [Vocadito arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.05580)
- HumTrans (Liu, Li, Li & Shan, 2023-24, arXiv; checked from PDF) [P]: ground-truth notes are the reference score converted to MIDI (via MuseScore). Hummers (10 music-major college students) hummed a displayed melody, with the paired MIDI taken as ground truth and no separate manual note annotation. The authors report that out-of-tune humming was a recording-time check. This means the labels encode the intended pitch from the score, not measured pitch. I did not extract the exact collection-time rejection criterion. — HumTrans PDF (read locally from a cached fetch; I did not confirm the arXiv ID or exact URL or the year/venue, so look these up before citing)
- MIR-ST500 (Wang & Jang, ICASSP 2021): about 160k notes in 500 pop songs, labeled by non-experts under set criteria, with onset labels admitted to be somewhat inconsistent [S]. The exact pitch-rounding convention was not confirmed. — [Wang & Jang, ICASSP 2021 PDF](https://code.soundsoftware.ac.uk/attachments/download/2806/ON%20THE%20PREPARATION%20AND%20VALIDATION%20OF%20A%20LARGE-SCALE%20DATASET%20OF%20SINGING%20TRANSCRIPTION.pdf) (fetch failed locally; search snippet only)
- Molina, Barbancho, Tardon & Barbancho (2014, ISMIR, pp. 567-572) [S]: evaluation framework and 1154 s cross-annotated dataset for singing transcription; there is no standard evaluation methodology. — [ISMIR 2014 paper](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2014/paper/000298.pdf)
- Wager et al. (2019, arXiv 1902.00956, "Deep Autotuner") [P]: they state that "different levels of sharpness or flatness from note to note ... make it difficult to know exactly what pitch the singer intended". Their mean validation loss was 0.123, which they convert to about 35 cents, on a task limited to ≤100 cent shifts. — [Deep Autotuner arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00956)

### Inferences
- The ±19-25 cent mean absolute error of non-poor singers, plus context biases of 15-30 cents, means a 10-30 cent deviation from the nearest equal-tempered pitch is routine, expected behavior and cannot on its own be evidence of a pitch error.
- The Vocadito disagreement on short notes and ornaments (0.64 F, and F=0.20 for naive quantization) means that for short notes even the existence and identity of the note is not agreed by humans. Short-note labels cannot be treated as an audio-only ground truth.
- HumTrans-style labels are score-derived. A trained model learns to map what was hummed to what was intended, rather than to what was measured.

### Gaps
- MIR-1K and MIR-ST500 pitch-deviation statistics (cents of measured pitch relative to the labels) were not found. Statistics specific to humming (as opposed to singing) accuracy in cents were not found.
- Scooping, portamento extent and onset-glide statistics for untrained singers were not retrieved (only qualitative references).
- Dai & Dixon units and the 2019 JASA figures should be checked against the full text.

## 3. How do note-level pitch-correction systems decide the intended pitch?

### Takeaway
Commercial tools do not infer the intended pitch from audio alone. They rely on a user-supplied key or scale, a score or MIDI, or manual per-note choice, with equal-tempered semitone snapping as the default. Retune speed and "humanize" or pitch-center and drift controls trade correction strength against naturalness. Academic work (Deep Autotuner, Tony) either learns a correction from the accompaniment or leaves the note pitch to a human.

### Cited Findings
- Per Wager et al. 2019 [P, describing Auto-Tune]: Auto-Tune measures F0 of monophonic singing and resynthesizes. Each note is automatically shifted to the nearest note in a user-input set of pitches (scale), or to the pitch in the score if known. Manually, an engineer moves each note to the desired score and precise pitch. The default scale is 12-tone equal temperament. They contrast this with their own approach, which has no score or scale, and note that commercial tools are not easily adaptable to other musical-cultural contexts with different scales or fluid pitch. — [Deep Autotuner arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00956)
- Auto-Tune controls [S, from Auto-Tune 5 / Access manuals and reviews, not Pro 11 docs]: Retune Speed (ms) sets how quickly the pitch is pulled to the target. A zero setting suppresses vibrato and pitch deviation entirely. Humanize applies a slower retune only to sustained notes so short notes still snap quickly. The recommended workflow is to tune retune speed to the shortest problem notes first. Target notes come from the key and scale settings. Presets quoted in one source (3, 25 and 65 ms) were inconsistent between sources and I could not resolve this. — [Auto-Tune 5 manual page](https://manualshive.com/en/manual/antares/auto-tune-5/2946798?page=32), [Orphiq explainer](https://orphiq.com/resources/what-is-autotune)
- Melodyne (Celemony Melodyne 5 help center) [P for the quoted text]: "Pitch modulation" is "rapid and usually intentional variations in pitch such as trills or vibrato". "Pitch drift" is "slow fluctuations in pitch of the kind that are usually unintentional". Percentages: 100% original, 0% a straight line, -100% inverted. Removing both yields "an unnaturally flat monotone". Celemony's design therefore assumes intentional versus unintentional variation can be separated by speed of change. Pitch-center correction [S, third-party tutorials]: moves each note toward the nearest semitone, or the nearest note of a chosen key/scale, by a user-set percentage (about 80% suggested as a starting point in some tutorials; the tutorials disagree). — [Celemony help: Pitch modulation and drift](https://helpcenter.celemony.com/M5/doc/melodyneStudio5/en/M5tour_ToolModulationDrift_2), [Mastering.com Melodyne tutorial](https://mastering.com/melodyne/)
- Tony (Mauch, Cannam, Bittner, Fazekas, Salamon, Dai, Bello & Dixon, 2015, "Computer-aided melody note transcription using the Tony software: accuracy and efficiency", TENOR 2015) [S]: automatic note pitch is the median of the pitch track over each note's duration. The user can split, merge or delete notes. Tony allows continuous pitch, so notes are not snapped to a grid. — [Tony page](https://sonicvisualiser.org/tony), [Tony bib](https://www.sonicvisualiser.org/tony/tony.bib.txt)
- Deep Autotuner (Wager et al., 2019) [P]: it assumes "a singer has one intended pitch for every note", with de-tuning constant within a note. It trains on 4,702 Smule karaoke performances selected for good intonation, de-tuned by synthetic note-wise shifts of up to 100 cents, and predicts the correction from the vocal and accompaniment spectra. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00956)

### Inferences
- Every deployed system resolves the intended-pitch ambiguity with outside information: a key or scale, a score, or user choice. None infers it from the isolated sung audio. This is consistent with Section 1 and 2: the audio does not determine the target.
- Retune speed and Humanize are a practical admission that short notes need a different treatment from long notes; this is a fact about the product design, not evidence of the ambiguity itself.

### Gaps
- Official Auto-Tune Pro documentation was not retrieved. Melodyne's own text on how pitch-center chooses a target (semitone versus scale) was not found in the official manual. Dai & Dixon work specifically on correction (rather than analysis) was not found.

## 4. Published methods that estimate "intended" pitch for untrained singers

### Takeaway
Published approaches estimate a latent reference pitch that is allowed to move (tonic regression, reference-pitch memory models, adaptive tuning in transcribers), use relative interval rather than absolute pitch, or learn a note-wise correction from reference accompaniment. I found no method that recovers the exact intended pitch of an isolated short note from audio alone.

### Cited Findings
- Mauch et al. 2014 [P]: with no external reference in unaccompanied singing, the reference is a linear fit through the per-note "implied tonic" estimates over each run of 25 notes. They state "no single best way of defining intonation is possible", and that interval and pitch errors indicate deviation from the equal-tempered grid, "not an esthetic judgment". They propose a causal model in which the reference pitch is a changing latent variable updated from the previous reference, and show that neither a static memory model nor a memoryless interval-based model explains the data. — [Mauch et al. 2014 PDF](https://webspace.eecs.qmul.ac.uk/s.e.dixon/pub/2014/JASA_136_1_401_1.pdf)
- Also in Mauch et al. 2014 [P, citing Ryynänen 2004]: Ryynänen built adaptive tuning into a note transcription system because "non-professional singers tend to change their tuning (typically downwards) during long melodies". Intonation drift was treated there as a nuisance factor. — same PDF
- Wager et al. 2019 [P]: learns a note-wise center-frequency shift from the vocal/accompaniment relationship. — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.00956)
- Interval-based ("relative pitch") evaluation: Mauch et al. use both interval error and pitch error; Dai & Dixon use MAPE and mean absolute melodic interval error (MAMIE). Ensemble context changed MAMIE by a few cents, for example about 3 cents between conditions. — [Dai & Dixon 2017](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2017/paper/000024.pdf)
- Ryynänen & Klapuri (2006, ISMIR, "Transcription of the Singing Melody in Polyphonic Music") confirmed as a source for note-event models with tuning handling, but I did not verify the details [U]. — [ISMIR 2006](https://archives.ismir.net/ismir2006/paper/000005.pdf)
- Vocadito shows that human annotators work from the "what I would play on a piano" criterion (a semitone-grid concept) while storing continuous pitch; the intended note is a human judgement. — [Vocadito arXiv PDF](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2110.05580)

### Inferences
- Intended-pitch estimation in the literature is always relative to something (a drifting tonic, a score, an accompaniment, or a human annotator). For repair of a short note, plausible external evidence is the neighboring notes' tuning, the local tonic estimate, and any reference/score available. This is my synthesis and not a published claim.

### Gaps
- I did not find the specific Molina et al. singing-transcription work that handles tuning (my Molina search returned only the 2014 evaluation framework). Molina's relative-pitch or tuning-correction contributions (for example in the Molina 2015 and 2016 work) remain unverified.
- No published method evaluated specifically on 10-30 cent repair of short notes was found.
- Other items I could not cover: MIR-1K details, Pfordresher & Brown 2007 primary text, and Dalla Bella pitch-stability numbers beyond the 0.3 semitone figure mentioned in Mauch et al.
